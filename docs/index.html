What the design takes from it

The model is an original design that works the same way, adapted for a motorcycle wheel.

One-way ratchet head

Open-top housing with a floor-rooted, two-tooth pawl. Load pushes the pawl into the teeth, so it locks tighter under tension. 45° lead-in, 90° lock shelf.

Stepped cleats

Full-width lugs with a lower and an upper step, filleted bases and rounded tops, so they resist shearing on the road.

Oval flex dishes

Shallow ovals between the cleats, on a thin 2.5 mm band that flexes around the tyre.

Tyre-side grip dots

Raised 4 mm domes (fewer, taller and wider than before) on the underside press into the tread rubber from every direction without cutting it.

Printed on edge

Coiled upright on the bed so teeth, pawl and cleats lie in the layer plane, the strongest direction for FDM.

Five per print

Plate files pack five straps (or six test pieces) onto one 330 × 270 mm bed for Onyx on a Markforged X7.

Gradual ramps

Smooth transitions from cleat band to strap, and a rounded tail, avoid stress concentrations.

How the lock works

A cut through the head at mid-width, and where the strap sits on each Hunter 350 tyre.

Pressure on each tooth

The lock has two pawl teeth that share the pull. Each overlaps the pawl by 0.75 mm over a 14.5 mm width (10.9 mm² bearing face).

PA12 tensile strength is about 45 MPa, so the tooth-root fillet at 39 MPa sets the rating of about 300 N per strap. If one tooth takes the whole load, bearing pressure doubles to 27.6 MPa and the local fillet peak reaches about 87 MPa, so that tooth yields and passes the load to the other. Bearing and shear are force ÷ area; the fillet peak is the CalculiX result at 300 N, scaled with load.

Torque handling by Royal Enfield model

Engine torque does not set the load on snow, because the tyre slips first. The traction limit does: force = μ × rear wheel load, with μ = 0.35 for packed snow and 0.15 for ice, the rear wheel carrying 55 % of bike plus a 75 kg rider.

  • On snow every model needs about the same wheel torque, 145–190 Nm. A 650 does not load the strap much harder than a 350, because the extra engine torque just spins the wheel.
  • With one strap carrying the whole pull, the Interceptor 650 and Super Meteor 650 reach or pass the 300 N rating (shown in red). The 350s and the Himalayan use 88–95 %.
  • With two straps in the contact patch, every model stays at or below about 55 % of the rating. That is why you fit 5–6 straps per wheel.
  • Braking loads the front straps the same way, up to μ × front wheel load.

Presets exist for the Hunter 350 Metro and Retro. The other models show the load only; add their tyre to the CAD script to build a strap for them. The friction values, wrap angle (4.5 rad) and weight split are assumptions, so treat the figures as rough, not as safe limits. They are static loads; bumps and hard braking add short peaks.

  • How: the plate coils are wound tighter (53–55 mm start radius, 43 mm or more at the tightest turn, so the core bends at most 2.8 %, near the 2.7 % the pawl already sees). Each outline is rotated and tucked beside its neighbours.
  • Print it: import the STL as it is, do not let the slicer re-orient it, use plain Onyx with solid fill, start at 0.1 mm layers, and keep the Onyx dry.
  • Strength: about 250 N per strap if scaled from PA12 by Onyx's lower tensile strength. That is an estimate, not an FEM run or a load test. With 5–6 straps per wheel the load per strap stays below it.
  • Lighter: the _LITE plates use about 8 % less Onyx (a Metro rear strap is about 41 g instead of 44 g) with the lock unchanged: tunnels through the cleats and a 10 mm strap where it never enters the head. Files are in out/lite/plates.
  • Check first: print the coupon plate and pull-test it warm and cold before trusting a set.

Best filament for strong traction, cold or warm

For long rides from a freezing winter morning to a hot summer road, use unfilled PA12 nylon.

RankMaterialWhy
1PA12 nylon filament, dried, printed on edgeStays tough in the cold. Takes up very little moisture, so strength and stiffness stay close to constant from damp winter to dry summer. Keeps its shape at road-warm temperatures. The strength figures above are for this material.
1SLS / MJF PA12 from a print serviceThe same material with no layer lines or warping. The most consistent result, best if you want several straps that behave alike.
2PA6/66 copolymer nylonEasy to print and tough, but absorbs more moisture, so it is softer and weaker when damp.
3PA6 nylonHighest strength when bone-dry, but swings the most with humidity. In published tests, conditioned PA6 kept only 56 % of its dry strength, against about 15 % lost for PA12.
2Onyx (Markforged, carbon-filled nylon)Stiffer than PA12 with lower tensile strength (about 35–40 MPa), so expect about 250 N per strap instead of 300 N. Unlike carbon-nylon filaments it is reported to stretch far before breaking, which should cope with the 2.7 % pawl strain. It is a nylon, so keep it dry. Not tested here: pull-test the coupon first. See the print plates above.
AvoidCarbon- or glass-filled nylon filamentStiffer, but stretches very little before cracking. The pawl bends 2.7 % every time a tooth clicks past, and the teeth have small root fillets.
AvoidPLA, PETG, ABS, PCPLA and PETG crack in the cold, and PLA softens in a hot car or garage. ABS and PC are brittle at notches.
AvoidTPUToo soft to hold the lock.
  • Choosing a brand: pick an unfilled PA12 whose datasheet shows elongation at break of 20 % or more, or a notched impact figure.
  • Before printing: dry it 8–12 h at 70–80 °C and print straight from a dry box. Leave the printed strap in room air for a day or two before fitting.
  • On long rides: nylon creeps slowly under steady load, faster when hot. Re-tighten after the first kilometre and every 5–10 km, more often in warm weather.
  • The filament does not set the traction. Grip comes from the cleats biting into snow; nylon itself is slippery on ice.
